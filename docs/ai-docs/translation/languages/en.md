# English Translation Notes

## Elision and Default-Filling

Korean frequently omits the subject, especially in reflective, essay-style
prose. When restoring an omitted subject in English, do not default to the
generic "you" as a shortcut — this is the single most common failure mode
observed in past translations (e.g. "블로그를 시작하게 된 이유" incorrectly
rendered as "Why you started a blog" instead of "Why I started this blog").

Before filling in a missing subject, determine from surrounding context
whether the passage is:
- first-person reflection (memoir, personal essay) — the overwhelmingly
  common case in this blog's essay posts
- second-person address to the reader (instructional, "how-to" tone)
- an impersonal, generalized statement (definitions, aphorisms)

When restoring the subject would feel heavy-handed even after correct
identification, consider nominalization as an alternative to inserting a
pronoun: "블로그를 시작하게 된 이유" can become "The reason I started this
blog," "What led me to start this blog," or simply "Why I started this
blog" — these carry different shades of introspection versus explanation,
and the choice should track the tone of the surrounding paragraph, not
just grammatical correctness.

## Sentence Rhythm and Syntax

Korean essay prose often builds a single train of thought across several
clauses before resolving it — a discursive, accretive rhythm. Do not
mechanically break these into short declarative English sentences merely
because that reads as "cleaner" prose. Doing so flattens the writer's
thinking process into a list of conclusions.

At the same time, English prose tends to make logical relationships
(causation, concession, contrast) more explicit through connectives than
Korean does. Where a logical relationship is implied but not stated in
the Korean original, it is often appropriate — and more natural in
English — to make it explicit (e.g. inserting "yet," "even so," "which is
why") rather than reproducing the Korean's paratactic juxtaposition
verbatim.

## Register and Distance

Korean formality is marked morphologically (합니다체 vs 해요체 vs 반말).
English has no equivalent inflectional system; formality is instead
carried by vocabulary register (Latinate vs Germanic word choice) and
syntactic complexity. Do not treat a formal Korean sentence ending as a
license to reach for heavier Latinate vocabulary across the board —
overcorrecting this way produces bureaucratic or stilted English that
reads as more distant than the Korean original actually is.

## Rhetorical Tradition

This blog mixes technical/academic writing with personal essay. English
technical writing in this register tolerates first-person voice
("I chose X because...") more readily than a stricter academic register
would — do not passivize sentences ("X was chosen because...") purely out
of a reflex toward academic neutrality when the Korean original is
plainly personal and reflective.
