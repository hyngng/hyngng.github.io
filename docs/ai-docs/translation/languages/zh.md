# Chinese (Traditional) Translation Notes

## Elision and Default-Filling

Like Korean, Chinese tolerates omission of subjects and pronouns, but
Chinese organizes this differently: it is a topic-prominent language,
where a topic is established at the beginning of a sentence or paragraph
and then referred to implicitly across subsequent clauses. When
translating a long, discursive Korean sentence with an omitted subject,
consider first identifying the paragraph's topic and fronting it
explicitly, then allowing the topic to carry implicitly through
following clauses — this often reproduces the flow of the Korean
original more naturally than hunting for an equivalent subject pronoun
clause by clause.

## Tense and Aspect

Chinese has no morphological tense; temporal relationships are conveyed
through aspect markers (了, 过, 在) and time adverbials rather than verb
inflection. Pay close attention to reproducing the Korean original's
sense of temporal distance — particularly the retrospective "looking
back on this now" quality common in this blog's essay posts — through
appropriate aspect marking and adverbial cues, since this distinction
does not carry over automatically the way it might in an
inflected-language translation.

## Register and Literary Density

Formal Traditional Chinese academic prose (particularly in a Taiwanese
academic register) has a convention of incorporating four-character
idioms (成語) or classical/literary (文言) phrasing to convey gravitas.
Where the Korean original is engaging with Sinitic philosophical or
historical concepts, some use of this literary register can be
appropriate. However, do not let this literary density overwhelm
passages where the Korean original's tone is plain, personal, and
conversational — the essay's reflective register should not be
uniformly elevated into classical-sounding prose.

## Political and Historical Sensitivity

Where the Korean original discusses contested historical or territorial
topics (e.g. disputes over the historical affiliation of ancient
kingdoms), translate the original author's actual stated position and
tone exactly as written, including direct characterizations of positions
the author disagrees with. Do not soften, hedge, or omit content on the
basis of political sensitivity. The task is translation, not editorial
mediation.
