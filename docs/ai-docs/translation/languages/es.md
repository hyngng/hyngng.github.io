# Spanish Translation Notes

## Elision and Default-Filling

Spanish is a pro-drop language, much like Korean — subjects do not need
to be stated explicitly when they are recoverable from verb conjugation
and context. Do not mechanically insert an explicit subject pronoun (yo,
uno, tú) every time Korean omits one; doing so often makes the Spanish
translation read as more emphatic or personally assertive than the
Korean original intended. Restore an explicit subject only when Spanish
grammar or clarity genuinely requires it, not by default.

## Sentence Rhythm and Syntax

Spanish essayistic prose is comfortable with layered subordinate clauses
— a rhythm closer to Korean's own discursive, accretive sentence
structure than English is. When translating a long, meditative Korean
sentence, there is usually no need to break it into shorter English-style
sentences first and then translate; a Spanish sentence built from nested
subordinate clauses can often carry the original's unbroken train of
thought more faithfully.

## Register and Distance

The tú/usted (and vosotros/ustedes) distinction should follow whatever
regional standard has been fixed for this project (European vs Latin
American Spanish), but note that reflective first-person essay prose
rarely triggers second-person address in the first place — do not
introduce "tú" constructions merely because the source text has a
conversational tone; conversational tone in Korean essay writing is
usually still first-person introspection, not direct address.

Also be aware of tense-usage conventions that differ between Peninsular
and Latin American Spanish (e.g. preference for pretérito perfecto vs
pretérito indefinido for recently completed past actions). Apply the
convention consistent with the fixed regional standard uniformly across
the piece.

## Rhetorical Tradition

Spanish-language essay writing tolerates open-ended, exploratory
sentence structures (planteamientos) that build toward a conclusion
rather than stating it upfront — this maps reasonably well onto the
Korean original's habit of arriving at a thesis only at the end of a
paragraph. Do not restructure paragraphs to front-load conclusions in a
way the Korean original does not.
