# Russian Translation Notes

## Elision and Default-Filling

Russian tolerates impersonal (subjectless) constructions far more
naturally than English does. When a subject is omitted in the Korean
original, do not automatically restore an explicit pronoun (я, ты, etc.)
as English-style translation habits might suggest. In many cases, a
Russian impersonal construction preserves the reflective, contemplative
tone of the Korean original more faithfully than inserting "я" would.

## Aspect Selection

Korean has no morphological marking equivalent to the Russian
perfective/imperfective distinction, so aspect must be inferred from
context every time a verb is translated — this is a more frequent source
of error than gender, number, or case agreement, and deserves equal
attention. Ask: is this a single completed action (perfective), or a
repeated, ongoing, or background state (imperfective)? In retrospective
essay narration, watch especially for habitual past actions ("~하곤
했다," "~던 시절") which typically require the imperfective, versus a
single decisive event ("~하게 되었다") which typically requires the
perfective. Getting this wrong changes the temporal texture of the
passage even when every word is otherwise correctly translated.

## Sentence Rhythm and Syntax

Russian word order is considerably freer than English, and this freedom
is itself a rhetorical tool: moving an element to the front of the
sentence is a natural way to mark emphasis. Korean marks emphasis through
particles and sentence-final position; do not default to reproducing
English-style SVO order out of habit when Russian word order could
reposition the emphasized element more naturally.

## Register and Distance

Academic/formal Russian prose has a strong tradition of nominalization
(отглагольное существительное — turning verbs into abstract nouns), which
can lend gravitas but, applied indiscriminately, produces the flavor of
Soviet-era bureaucratic prose. In personal essay passages, balance this
against verb-centered constructions that carry the writer's individual
voice — do not nominalize every sentence simply because the source
register is formal.

## Rhetorical Tradition

Where the Korean original addresses a historically or politically
contested topic, preserve its measured, multi-perspective tone exactly —
Russian-language discourse on adjacent geopolitical topics can carry its
own charged associations, but the translation's job is to carry the
original author's restraint, not to recalibrate it for a different
audience's expectations.
