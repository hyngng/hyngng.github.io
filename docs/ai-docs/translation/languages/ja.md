# Japanese Translation Notes

## Elision and Default-Filling

Japanese, like Korean, omits subjects extremely naturally — arguably even
more readily than Korean does in reflective essay prose. Do not restore
an explicit subject (私は, 自分は) merely because the Korean original
happens to include one, or out of a reflex to make the sentence
"complete." Over-restoring explicit subjects in Japanese produces prose
that reads as self-conscious or overly assertive; native Japanese essay
writing sustains long subjectless passages far more comfortably than
either Korean or English does.

## Sentence-Final Form (文末表現) and Tone

The choice of sentence-ending form — だ/である体 versus ます体, or
ending a sentence on a bare noun (体言止め) — governs the essay's
perceived tone (assertive, tentative, reflective) more than almost any
other single choice in Japanese. Where the Korean original hedges or
trails off into uncertainty ("~일지도 모른다," "~아닐까 싶다"), do not
collapse this into a flat である/だ assertion; Japanese has its own
resources for preserving tentativeness (かもしれない, ではないだろうか,
のだろうか) and these should be used to match the Korean original's
epistemic stance, not smoothed over into false certainty.

Decide up front whether である体 (formal, essayistic/academic) or だ体
(more personal, conversational-formal) is the default register for this
blog's essay genre, and apply it consistently — do not let it drift
between paragraphs within the same post.

## Kanji Density and Register

Korean and Japanese share a large stock of Sino-Korean/Sino-Japanese
vocabulary (漢字語), but frequency and formality do not map one-to-one.
A Sino-Korean term translated with its cognate Sino-Japanese kanji
compound can land as more archaic, legalistic, or academically stiff in
Japanese than the Korean original felt in Korean. Where this mismatch
occurs, consider whether a native Japanese (和語) phrasing would better
match the register of a personal essay, rather than defaulting to the
kanji-compound cognate simply because it exists and is technically
correct.

## Rhetorical Tradition

Japanese essay writing (随筆, zuihitsu-adjacent personal essay
tradition) tolerates a meandering, associative structure that arrives at
its point gradually and often leaves it partially unstated. This maps
well onto the Korean original's discursive style — do not impose a more
Western-essay structure (explicit thesis, topic sentence, resolution) on
paragraphs where the Korean original itself resists that structure.
